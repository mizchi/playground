**VERDICT: better**

The extractions map to real, reused concepts rather than cosmetic splitting: `roundCents` kills a duplicated formula, `tieredRate` unifies three near-identical ascending-threshold lookups (member discount, gold discount, shipping cost) into one table-driven pattern, `subtotalOf`/`totalWeight` are each called from two call sites, and `summarizeBy` in report.ts collapses three copy-pasted loops into one. The validation split (`validateUser`/`validateItem`/`validateItems`/`validateCoupon`) gives each rule a name without changing behavior, and I checked the shipping tier boundary math (`>=` descending order vs. the original `<` ascending chain) — it's behavior-preserving at every boundary (0.5, 1, 5, 20). This isn't just chasing a duplication/complexity score; the resulting names read close to the business rules (free shipping, tiered rate, category weight).

Two real smells worth fixing, though:

1. **Misleading parameter names in `tieredRate`**: it's declared as `tieredRate(years: number, tiers: Array<[minYears: number, rate: number]>)`, but it's also called with `weight` for shipping (`tieredRate(weight, tiers)`). The generic helper still carries "years" naming baked in from its first use case — a maintainer skimming `baseShippingRate` has to notice the mismatch to trust it's correct. Rename the params to something domain-neutral (`x`, `minX`) since the function is genuinely generic.
2. **Undocumented ordering contract**: `tieredRate` assumes its `tiers` array is sorted descending by threshold and returns on first match — that invariant isn't stated anywhere (no comment, no sort-on-read, no assertion). Someone adding a tier to `SHIPPING_RATE_TIERS` or `CATEGORY_UNIT_WEIGHT`-style tables out of order gets silently wrong results with no compile-time or runtime signal. This is the one place I'd ask for a one-line comment or a defensive sort before shipping the refactor.

Neither of these is "gaming" a metric (no pointless function-splitting just to lower per-function complexity counts, no trivial one-line extractions solely to boost a reuse score) — they're a side effect of a legitimately good abstraction that wasn't fully cleaned up after generalizing.
